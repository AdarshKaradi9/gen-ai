If you build one: the screen-time companion. It has the highest score and the most proven money. It's also the most crowded, so it only works with the accountability-group angle described below and one sharp audience.

A pattern worth noticing. Three of the top seven are about belief and identity: astrology, faith and manifesting. This was the strongest area in the whole study, and it was absent from round one.

How this was done

The filter. The earlier research on 110 videos found the same six traits in apps that spread and earned. Every idea was scored 1 to 5 on each of them, for a total out of 30.

  1. Two-second aha. Can the core value be shown in a two-second screen recording?
  2. Video hook. Is there a natural short-video demo or transformation?
  3. Money already flows. Do people already pay competitors or spend on the problem?
  4. Reason to return. Is there a daily or weekly unit for a streak? For business tools, this is a recurring weekly job.
  5. Shareable moment. Is there something users would screenshot or send?
  6. Unit economics. Can revenue per install plausibly beat the cost of getting one?

The research. Seventeen research agents covered 59 ideas in 11 areas: health and body, discipline, mind and spirit, camera utilities, sports and style, creators and small business, money and career, social, learning and kids, travel, and home and family. Together they ran about 230 web searches. They read revenue trackers, verified revenue listings, store pages, review analyses, press and studies.

How to read the numbers. Each figure is labelled by how much to trust it:

  • Verified means revenue connected through RevenueCat or Stripe and published on TrustMRR or whatsthe.app. This is the strongest signal.
  • Third-party estimate means trackers like AppGoblin, Appfigures, Screensdesign, Sensor Tower or trends.vc. These are useful for scale but can be off by several times. Different trackers often disagree.
  • Self-reported means founders talking about their own apps.

Limits.

  • Scores came from different agents, so a gap of one or two points isn't meaningful.
  • Reddit blocked page reads, so some community sizes are missing.
  • Most review evidence comes through review analyses rather than direct scrapes.
  • One correction from round two: ReciMe's revenue is only estimated, at $100K to $1M a month. No source confirms the $500K to $800K figure used earlier.

1. Screen-time companion: 27/30

The pitch. A screen-time blocker with a character that wilts when you doomscroll and recovers when you don't. A "years of your life back" counter makes the cost feel real, and a small accountability group makes the blocker hard to quietly switch off.

Money proof. - Opal: about $400K a month (Adapty, third-party), and 2024 revenue is listed at $4.8M. - Brainrot: a cartoon-brain pet blocker launched in mid-2025, reportedly at $400K a month within a year (self-reported). - Brick: sells a $59 physical blocker and claims "multiple eight figures" (company claim).

What users hate. A review analysis of five leading blockers, published by a competitor, grouped the complaints:

Complaint Share
Surprise charge when the trial ends 24%
Too easy to bypass 19%
Blocking silently breaks after iOS updates 16%
Guilt mechanics backfire 12%
Worked for a week, then stopped 10%

The angle to win. - Accountability instead of willpower. Unlocking a blocked app at 1am notifies one or two chosen friends, which also builds in invites. - A character that heals, not just rots. Brainrot only punishes. A companion that recovers, plus a lost day you can earn back, fixes the guilt complaint. - Honest billing. A reminder before the trial charges and an annual price under $40. - One audience first. College students in finals season, with a shareable count of study hours.

The two-second clip. TikTok opens and the character wilts under "-4.2 years of your life at this pace". A week later the same character glows over "You got back 11h 40m this week".

Paywall. At the end of onboarding: $34.99 a year preselected, a 3-day trial, and the charge date stated.

Risks. It's the most crowded category in the top seven. Every iOS blocker depends on Apple's fragile Screen Time API, and Apple's own Screen Time is free.

Sources: Adapty on Opal, Lazyweb on Opal, Stork.ai on Brainrot, Entrepreneur on Brick, Unstar review analysis

2. Astrology with your friends: 25/30

The pitch. A daily AI astrology reading about you and the five people in your group chat. Each morning you get your own card plus one line about a named friend ("text Jess, avoid Priya"), and you can pull a compatibility card for any pair in the group.

Money proof. - Nebula: about $500K to $550K a month (Screensdesign, and AppMagic via Surfshark, third-party). It sells a subscription after a 26-step quiz, then birth-chart upsells and paid psychic chat. - The category: the average astrology app earns about $125K a month (Screensdesign). - A small AI fortune-telling app: $64K a month, verified on TrustMRR. - Co-Star: about 4.3 million monthly users, but under $10K a month on Android. Midjourney bought it in July 2026, which leaves room for a fresher product.

Who. US women aged 18 to 30 who already know their sun, moon and rising signs and use astrology as social language. A 2024 Harris Poll found 62% of Gen Z and 83% of millennials at least somewhat believe in it.

The angle to win. Make the friend group the product. Co-Star has friend charts, but its daily reading is still solo. Here the daily card names a friend, and a group "cosmic weather" card ranks who is having the best day. Every friend who adds a birth time makes the readings better, so invites become the core of the app.

The two-second clip. A group-chat screenshot next to a card reading "Today: text Jess, avoid Priya, Leo moon energy is unhinged". Then a swipe to a 92% compatibility card.

Paywall. At the end of onboarding: $39.99 a year with a 7-day trial, or $6.99 a week. The free tier keeps the daily card and one friend, so the invite loop isn't broken. Unlimited friends and deep pair reports are paid, with a one-time $9.99 pair report as a second offer.

Risks. - Nebula outspends everyone on ads. - Co-Star's free tier anchors the price at zero. - A user whose friends don't join will churn. - Holding friends' birth data raises privacy questions.

Sources: Screensdesign astrology category, Screensdesign on Nebula, Statista on top horoscope apps, Co-Star on AppGoblin, Surfshark on astrology app privacy

3. Daily faith habit: 25/30

The pitch. A two-minute daily faith practice: one verse, one reflection tied to what you're going through, and one short prayer. The streak is shared with three to five friends. It's built for young US Protestant and non-denominational Christians, who find Hallow too Catholic and YouVersion too much like homework.

Money proof. - Hallow: about $40M net in 2025 (Appfigures, third-party). It saw 263,000 downloads on Ash Wednesday 2026, about 25 times a normal day. - Bible Chat: founders claim $15M a year (self-reported), and over 95% of its revenue is from the US. - Bibly: a solo-built Bible app at $4.3K a month from about 1,000 subscribers (TrustMRR, verified).

The angle to win. Make the daily act a small-group ritual: a group streak, a shared prayer-request wall, and youth-pastor group codes for distribution. Add a Lent and Advent challenge for Protestants, who have no Hallow equivalent. Lent brings a predictable 40-day demand spike every year.

The two-second clip. Someone types "stressed about finals". A verse card animates in, then the group streak ticks from 41 to 42 as four friends' avatars light up.

Paywall. At the end of onboarding: $34.99 a year with a 7-day trial. The daily verse and group streak stay free.

Risks. - More than 9,000 Bible apps exist. - Free YouVersion sets the price anchor at zero. - AI reflections must quote scripture and avoid doctrinal claims. - Social features for under-18s need parental consent.

Sources: Appfigures on Hallow, Appfigures on Bible Chat, Bibly on TrustMRR

4. Glow-up through food: 25/30

The pitch. Log water, sugar, sleep and skin-friendly foods, take one guided selfie a day, and watch a timeline that shows what your skin did after your best and worst weeks. No face scores.

Money proof. - DailyGlowUp: launched in January 2026, it reached $13,537 a month in revenue within seven months, with $2.29 per download and a $116 lifetime value per paying user (RevenueCat-verified). It's now listed for sale at $450K. - Umax: the face-rating app reports about $500K a month (self-reported).

What users hate. In 1,559 one- and two-star reviews of 12 face-rating apps: - 67.6% complain about paying. - 31.1% say results stayed locked until they paid.

The angle to win. Show cause and effect, with no scores. For example: "Your three clearest days followed 2 litres of water and under 25g of added sugar", with matching selfies. Guided capture keeps the timeline honest, and one real insight comes before the paywall.

The two-second clip. A 30-day selfie timelapse going from red, bumpy cheeks to calm skin, with a strip of daily habits underneath. End frame: "Day 30. No new products. Just food."

Paywall. After the plan and one free insight: $39.99 a year, or a weekly option.

Risks. - The audience skews teen, so set an age rating of 17+ and never rate attractiveness. - Evidence that diet clears skin is modest, so present it as tracking, not treatment.

Sources: DailyGlowUp on whatsthe.app, appearance-app review analysis, Umax on the App Store

5. Photo-to-quote for trades: 25/30

The pitch. A tradesperson snaps two or three photos on site, says one sentence ("replace 40 feet of fence, cedar"), and sends the client a branded, itemised quote before getting back in the van.

Money proof. - Jobber: about $167.5M revenue in 2024 (Latka, third-party). It claims 200,000 home-service pros. - Handoff: an AI estimating tool that raised $5.8M in 2025 and says 40,000 contractors use it (self-reported). - Price tolerance: trades already pay for apps in this space. One App Store reviewer reports a $79 charge.

What users hate. Reviews of a leading AI estimator call it "very, very inaccurate". One says it gave the same price for jobs needing 12 and 30 yards of concrete, and that "if you rely on this, this app will 100% make you lose work".

The angle to win. Don't guess prices from the internet. Learn the contractor's own price book from three past quotes, then use photos and voice only for scope and quantities, with every line editable. Start with one trade where photos map cleanly to quantities, such as interior painting. Make the client's PDF beautiful, with e-signature and a deposit link, and ask for a review after the job.

Why it spreads. Every quote goes to a homeowner with a small "Made with" footer. Homeowners hire other trades, so each quote is free distribution.

The two-second clip. The phone points at a living-room wall, the shutter clicks, and an itemised quote ("2 coats, 420 sq ft, trim, $1,840") lands on the client's phone with an Accept button. Caption: "I quoted this job before leaving the driveway."

Paywall. After the first quote is sent: $29 a month or $199 a year, framed as "one won job pays for the year".

Risks. - A bad quote costs the tradesperson real money, so the AI must assist, not decide. - Funded players exist, and Jobber can add AI. - It needs to work alongside QuickBooks and Jobber rather than replace them.

Sources: Handoff funding announcement, Latka on Jobber, BetaKit on Jobber, Cost Estimator - Construction+ on the App Store

6. Manifest widget: 24/30

The pitch. A home-screen affirmation widget tied to one goal you're manifesting. Each night you log one "sign" that something went your way, so the app builds a visible case that your luck is changing.

Money proof. These are third-party estimates. - I am: $200K+ a month, 71% from ads, with about 2.5 million monthly users (AppGoblin). Screensdesign puts it at $750K. It has 1.1 million ratings and has been earning since 2014. - Motivation: about $450K a month. - Stella, Manifest Anything: about $350K a month on only 55,000 installs, which suggests a hard paywall converts well here. - Aya: about $100K a month.

The angle to win. Make manifesting feel like it's working. The user names one goal, and the AI writes affirmations for that goal to rotate in the widget. A ten-second nightly "sign log" fills a jar of evidence, and a monthly "manifestation receipt" card gives something to share. The leaders are ad-heavy quote feeds with no memory of the user's goal and no sense of progress.

The two-second clip. A lock-screen widget reads "I am the obvious hire for this role". Cut to a jar filling with 23 glowing signs and a card: "Day 23: got the interview".

Paywall. A hard paywall after the widget preview: $29.99 a year with a 3-day trial, plus a lifetime option, and no ads.

Risks. - A portfolio studio with many near-identical apps leads the market, and Apple penalises template clones. - The "lucky girl" trend peaked in 2023, though the genre has lasted twelve years. - Never promise health or wealth outcomes.

Sources: I am on AppGoblin, Screensdesign on I am, Monkey Taps on AppBrain, App Masters on Motivation

7. Recipe saver from social video: 24/30

The pitch. Share any TikTok, Reel or YouTube Short and get a clean recipe card, a cook mode and a merged grocery list. It works even when the recipe was only spoken aloud.

Money proof. - ReciMe: 401,000 iOS ratings at 4.77 stars, gaining about 5,700 a week, and about 1.6 million iOS monthly users (AppGoblin). Revenue is estimated at $100K to $1M a month (third-party, wide range). It raised a $1.5M seed. - Honeydew: under $10K a month. Being in the category doesn't guarantee revenue.

What users hate. - Imports fail or come out altered for some users. - ReciMe takes only one recipe per post. - Users complain about aggressive subscription pushes and ask for a lifetime option.

The angle to win. Be the most reliable on the hardest inputs: videos where the recipe is only spoken, posts with several recipes, and captions in other languages. Add a lifetime purchase and a Sunday "plan my week" that turns a pile of saves into five dinners and one grocery list. Indian or Korean cooking, where measurements are loose, is a second angle.

The two-second clip. Tap Share on a chaotic 40-second TikTok with no written recipe. One second later a tidy card appears with 8 ingredients and 5 steps. Caption: "It even caught the stuff she only said out loud."

Paywall. A soft paywall after the third import: $34.99 a year, or $79 lifetime.

Risks. - Pulling from TikTok and Instagram may break their terms, and they can block it. - ReciMe already owns the category. - Video processing costs more per import than parsing text.

Test before building. Check whether your importer beats ReciMe on 20 hard TikToks.

Sources: ReciMe on AppGoblin, Adapty on ReciMe, Screensdesign on ReciMe, SmartCompany on ReciMe

What the winners have in common

  • Belief, identity and appearance monetise best. Astrology, faith, manifesting, glow-up and screen-time guilt all earn. Practical life admin mostly doesn't: travel, chores, friendships, subscriptions, salary and taxes.
  • Business tools beat consumer tools where the job repeats every week. Photo-to-quote scored at the top. Gig-driver tax and review-reply tools scored low, because free or built-in options exist.
  • A big audience doesn't mean big money. Co-Star, Locket, Gas, Muslim Pro, Daily Dozen and Zola all have huge audiences and earn little or rely on ads. The earners use hard paywalls at the end of onboarding.
  • Every category got a 2025 to 2026 clone wave. Speed and a real angle matter more than the idea.
  • Honest billing is an angle almost everywhere. Surprise trial charges were a top complaint in screen-time, glow-up, ADHD, recipe and baby apps.
  • One-and-done results don't hold subscriptions. Colour analysis, dating profile review, jet lag, salary negotiation and subscription audits each solve something once. They need a one-time price or a daily loop added.

About your nutrient body map

It scored 18 out of 30 and ranks 44th of 59. The tap-to-glow aha is excellent, and the body-map visual looks unclaimed. But no evidence shows people pay for nutrient tracking where users just tick off what they ate.

The research suggests three changes: - Narrow the audience to GLP-1 users or plant-based eaters. - Add a "30 plants a week" fiber row. - Charge about $35 a year at the end of onboarding.

How to start

  1. Pick one idea from the top seven.
  2. Test demand for two weeks before coding. Post the two-second clip daily as a mock-up and put up a landing page with a waitlist. Move on only if strangers ask where to get it.
  3. Build a four-week MVP following the playbook: the data model first, one aha, a paywall at the end of onboarding, and a streak with two freezes.
  4. Measure one number: whether the day-30 cohort curve flattens.

Every idea researched, with its score, verdict and pitch. Scores are out of 30 against the six traits described in the method section.

Health, food and body

Glow-Up Food · 25/30 · Build

Log water, sugar, sleep and skin-friendly foods, take one consistent daily selfie, and watch a timeline that shows what your skin did after your best and worst weeks.

Verdict: Build. Money and content format are proven by face-rating apps, and a verified small player (DailyGlowUp) shows a solo-scale path, while the top complaints (locked results, scam feel) point straight at an honest, score-free, diet-driven wedge.

Supplement Stack Map · 23/30 · Build

Scan every bottle in your cabinet and see your whole stack as a body coverage map, with overlaps, totals versus official upper limits, cost per day and a daily "taken" check-off.

Verdict: Build, cautiously. This has the best money evidence of the three, with verified solo-scale revenue and a large paying habit, and incumbents have clear trust and data complaints.

Quit Sugar · 23/30 · Maybe, leaning build

A "quit X" app for added sugar: a big days-sugar-free counter, an SOS button for cravings, money saved on sweets and drinks, and a body-recovery timeline ("day 3: cravings peak, day 10: taste buds reset"), sold with a hard onboarding paywall.

Verdict: Maybe, leaning build only as a fast, cheap test. The quit-X playbook is proven and incumbent sugar apps are buggy food loggers, so there is a real wedge, but no sugar app has yet shown real revenue and the shelf is filling with clones.

GLP-1 Off-Ramp · 22/30 · Maybe, leaning build

The app for the half of GLP-1 users who stop within a year or two: a guided taper-and-maintenance plan that tracks weight against a "keep-it-off band", protects muscle with protein and two short strength sessions a week, and logs the return of food noise so users act before the regain starts.

Verdict: Maybe, leaning build as a focused wedge. The money is proven in GLP-1 companions and the off-ramp is a large, growing, under-served moment backed by fresh regain studies, but willingness to pay among cost-driven quitters is unproven and incumbents can copy.

Desk Body · 22/30 · Maybe, leaning build

A 2-minute reset between meetings: the camera checks your neck and shoulder position, you do three guided moves and an eye break, and the camera shows the difference.

Verdict: Maybe, leaning build. The money is proven by Bend and the measured before/after gives a real demo and wedge, but the category is crowded with posture nags and Bend owns the ads.

Desi Plate · 22/30 · Maybe, leaning build

Snap your thali and get calories, protein, iron and B12 for dal, roti and sabzi in katori and roti units, with a one-tap oil/ghee prompt that fixes the biggest error source, built first for the South Asian diaspora.

Verdict: Maybe, leaning build only as a diaspora-first, protein-for-vegetarians product. The demand and the wedge (katori units plus oil prompt) are real, but the space is filling with small clones, and a well-funded incumbent (Healthify) is entering the US.

Step Pet · 20/30 · Maybe, leaning skip

A pet that only grows when you walk, so your daily step count becomes something you care for instead of a number you ignore.

Verdict: Maybe, leaning skip. The habit loop is excellent and Finch proves pets retain, but every small step-pet app we could measure earns pocket money, and the price ceiling is low.

Gut Fiber · 19/30 · Maybe, leaning skip

A fibermaxxing tracker built around the 30-plants-a-week goal: log each plant once, watch a weekly garden fill toward 30, see an estimated fiber total, with an optional private stool log.

Verdict: Maybe, leaning skip as a standalone. Demand and content fit are real and rising, but no fiber or plant-count app has shown meaningful revenue and 2026 clones are already piling in.

Caffeine Cutoff · 19/30 · Skip

Log a coffee in one tap and see a half-life curve showing how much caffeine will still be in you at bedtime, with a personal "last coffee" cutoff time and, over weeks, how late caffeine actually changes your sleep from Apple Health data.

Verdict: Skip as a standalone subscription business. The aha is excellent and makes a great free hook or a feature inside a broader sleep or energy app, but ten years of caffeine trackers show low revenue, users who prefer one-time payment, and insights that decay once learned.

Nutrient Body Map · 18/30 · Maybe

Tap the nutrients you paid attention to today and watch a body map light up the organs they support: a habit tracker for nutrition awareness with no calorie counting, no recommendations and cited fact cards.

Verdict: Maybe. The aha and habit loop are good and the body-map angle is unclaimed, but there is no evidence anyone pays for declare-only nutrient tracking and the closest loved app is free.

Peptide Stack Tracker · 18/30 · Skip

A tracker for people running injectable peptide stacks (BPC-157, TB-500, retatrutide, compounded GLP-1s): reconstitution math, vial inventory, site rotation, and a per-compound "is this doing anything?" view against sleep, weight and symptoms.

Verdict: Skip. The trend is real and loud, but the app layer saturated in 2026 (24 trackers, one excellent free one with about 1,900 ratings in four months), revenue per player is small, and the regulatory and ad-platform risk sits on the founder.

Cycle Sync · 15/30 · Skip

An app that tells you what to eat and how hard to train today based on your menstrual cycle phase, with phase-matched meals, workouts and a daily check-in.

Verdict: Skip. The trend is loud on TikTok but has not converted into revenue for dedicated apps (28 has 8K+ ratings yet under $10K/mo by estimate), the science is increasingly criticized, and Flo owns the user and the data.

Perimenopause Strength and Symptom Companion · 14/30 · Skip

A consumer app for women 40-55 that links daily symptom check-ins (sleep, hot flashes, mood, brain fog) with a simple protein and strength plan, showing which habits move which symptoms, without being a telehealth or HRT prescriber.

Verdict: Skip as a consumer subscription app. Demand and cultural momentum are real, but the dollars go to telehealth prescribers, the leading consumer app monetises at under $10K/month by estimate, and Apple and Flo are commoditising tracking in 2026.

Discipline and habits

Screen Time Companion · 27/30 · Maybe, leaning build

A screen-time blocker for one specific audience (college students in exam season, or parents who want to model less phone use for their kids) where a character's health and a "years of your life back" counter make the screen-time cost easy to feel, plus a small accountability group so the blocker is harder to quietly switch off.

Verdict: Maybe, leaning build. The demand and money are the strongest of the three, and the character format is already proven.

ADHD Focus · 22/30 · Maybe

An ADHD-friendly focus app that turns one dreaded task into three tiny steps, starts a 10-minute session with an always-available body double (a live stranger room or an AI companion character), and rewards finishing with a pet that grows, never a red "you failed" streak.

Verdict: Maybe. Willingness to pay is proven and incumbents have clear holes (aggressive billing, planning-first design, desktop body doubling), but the space is crowded with beloved brands and churn is structural.

Couples Health Habits · 20/30 · Maybe, leaning skip

A two-player habit app where partners set shared daily goals (a walk, a vegetable-first dinner, no late snacking), see each other's check-ins, keep a joint streak that only grows when both show up, and get nudged with "your partner is waiting on you".

Verdict: Maybe, leaning skip. Couples apps get organic reach and the built-in invite is a real growth loop, but proven revenue tops out near $10K MRR and nothing shows couples will pay for health habits specifically.

No-Spend Streak · 19/30 · Maybe

Quittr for shopping: a "days without an impulse buy" streak with a running "money kept" counter, an SOS screen for the moment you're about to check out, and a blocker for shopping apps (Shein, Temu, Amazon, TikTok Shop).

Verdict: Maybe, as a seasonal bet. The trend is real and the point-of-purchase blocker is a wedge no one has built.

Quit Betting · 18/30 · Skip

A Quittr-style app for young US men quitting sports betting: a "days bet-free" streak, a running "money not lost" counter, an SOS screen for game-day urges, and a one-tap blocker for sportsbook apps (DraftKings, FanDuel, prediction markets), timed to the NFL and NBA calendar.

Verdict: Skip as a lead consumer bet; maybe as a mission-driven side project or a B2B2C play. The need is growing and well documented by helpline data, but the monetisation proof is missing, paid acquisition is restricted, sharing is limited by shame, and a crowd of 2026 clones already exists.

Mind, spirit and self

Astrology Daily · 25/30 · Maybe, leaning build

A daily AI astrology reading that is about you and the five people in your group chat: each morning you get your own card plus one "who to text, who to avoid" line about a named friend, and a compatibility card for any pair in the group.
