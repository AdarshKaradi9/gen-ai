# Nutrient Body Map

## One-line pitch
Tap the nutrients you paid attention to today and watch a body map light up the organs they support: a habit tracker for nutrition awareness with no calorie counting, no recommendations and cited fact cards.

## Who exactly
The idea as written targets "anyone curious about nutrition", which is too broad to find a first thousand users. The sharpest beachheads the evidence supports:
- **Plant-based and vegetarian eaters** who worry about B12, iron, omega-3, zinc and calcium. r/vegan has about 1.9M-2.0M members (GummySearch cached snapshot, third-party). The Daily Dozen app already proves this crowd will check boxes daily.
- **GLP-1 users**, who are told to watch protein, fiber and water while eating less. trends.vc counts 20 paying GLP-1 companion apps, Shotsy at about $4.7M/yr (third-party estimate, main agent findings). This is the audience where money already swims.
- **Indian vegetarian home cooks** (from the playbook's distribution plan), reachable through cooking communities, but no revenue evidence was found for them.

## Demand evidence
- Dr. Greger's Daily Dozen, a free checkbox food-group tracker, has about 20K iOS ratings at 4.9 stars and roughly 45K MAU (AppGoblin, third-party estimate), with ~749K iOS installs claimed by one tracker (third-party). Declare-not-measure tracking clearly has a loyal audience.
- Google's Summergeist 2026 post says searches for dietary fiber hit an all-time high in 2026 and "fibermaxxing" rose 115% in 90 days (Google Trends via Google blog). Nutrient-specific curiosity is rising.
- 75% of US adults take supplements (CRN 2024 survey), suggesting broad nutrient-level anxiety.
- I could not find any app called "Holistic Loader" or "RootMap", nor any App Store app whose core is an organ body map that lights up from nutrient logging. The body-map visual appears unclaimed, which is both a gap and a warning that nobody has proven demand for it.

## Money proof
- Daily Dozen: free, no IAP, run by a non-profit (App Store listing). Its users are trained not to pay.
- Cronometer: Gold at $8.99/mo or $49.99/yr (pricing site, 2026). Revenue estimates conflict: about $3.8M (Latka) vs about $15M (techlist.ai), both third-party. People do pay for micronutrient depth, but they pay for measurement, which this idea explicitly rejects.
- Protein tracker apps: mostly under $10K/mo; one AI protein tracker sold for $50K at ~$7K ARR (main agent findings, third-party).
- GLP-1 companions: $81K/yr typical, Shotsy ~$4.7M (trends.vc, third-party), $49.99/yr.
- No evidence found that anyone pays for a declare-only nutrient checklist.

## Competitor weaknesses
Daily Dozen reviews (JustUseApp excerpts of App Store reviews): users cannot see their history or progress over time ("Please make it so we can see our past"), cannot edit the list ("I wish I could edit the list"), the redesign made each item take too much room and requires scrolling, the date sometimes sticks on yesterday, and there are no friends or sharing. The list is fixed to one doctor's whole-food vegan framework. Cronometer is the opposite problem: accurate but requires weighing and logging every food.

## The wedge
"Daily Dozen's simplicity, but for nutrients, for any diet, with history and a picture of your body." Concretely: an editable grid of 6-12 nutrients, one-tap logging that fits on one screen, real history and a heatmap (the top Daily Dozen request), and the body map as the visual reward nobody else has. What would most improve the odds:
1. **Audience:** launch as a GLP-1 or plant-based specialist, not general. A GLP-1 version ("protein, fiber, water, B12, muscle") lands in a market with 20 paying apps and $50/yr norms. A vegan version gets free community distribution.
2. **Wedge:** add the fiber/30-plants weekly counter as a built-in row to ride the 2026 fibermaxxing search wave.
3. **Monetization:** a free-forever grid competes with a free, beloved non-profit app. Put the paywall at the end of onboarding (soft, dismissible) and sell the weekly body report and history at $29.99-$39.99/yr; for a GLP-1 audience test a harder trial-led wall.

## The 2-second clip
A dark silhouette of a body. A thumb taps the "Iron" chip; the blood vessels pulse red and a caption reads "you touched iron today". Second tap on "Calcium", the skeleton glows white.

## Core loop sketch
- **Onboarding aha:** pick diet pattern, then "tap one you covered today", body lights up within 30 seconds (playbook Applied section).
- **Daily action:** 5-second evening check-in of nutrients touched, optional food tag.
- **Streak/reward:** daily goal of N nutrients, per-nutrient streaks, two freezes, day-7 summary ("muscles lit 6 days, bones once").
- **Shareable artifact:** weekly body map card, square and watermarked.
- **Paywall:** after the first lit map, annual preselected at about $34.99/yr with a 7-day trial; second showing at the day-7 summary.

## Risks
- **Retention:** declaring is easy to fake and easy to forget; the body map may be novel for a week and then static. No incumbent proves a body map retains.
- **Monetization:** the closest proof of daily use (Daily Dozen) is free, and paid demand sits with measurement apps.
- **Regulatory:** low. "Supports" claims about organs must quote authoritative sources (NIH ODS, EFSA) or Apple can flag uncited health content under guideline 1.4.1 (Apple developer forum reports). FDA's January 2026 general wellness guidance keeps low-risk wellness apps outside device rules.
- **Saturation:** low for the body-map angle, very high for nutrition tracking in general (Cal AI now owned by MyFitnessPal).

## Verdict
Maybe. The aha and habit loop are good and the body-map angle is unclaimed, but there is no evidence anyone pays for declare-only nutrient tracking and the closest loved app is free. Its odds improve most by narrowing to GLP-1 users (protein, fiber, water) or plant-based eaters, adding a 30-plants fiber row, and testing the paywall at onboarding rather than relying on freemium.
