## Core claims (3-7 bullets, what the video argues)
- (Video description) A 2013 keynote sponsored by Detroit Venture Partners on the psychology behind why certain products engage us, i.e. the Hook model that became the book Hooked (self-published Jan 2014, Portfolio Nov 2014).
- Companies' economic value is increasingly a function of the strength of the habits they create; "just amassing millions of users is no longer good enough."
- Habit-forming products attach to internal triggers (emotions, routines) so users self-cue without marketing: "first-to-mind wins."
- Startups "manufacture desire" by running users through Hooks: Trigger -> Action -> Variable Reward -> Investment; each pass makes the next trigger more likely.
- Variable (unpredictable) reward is what separates a Hook from a plain feedback loop (fridge-light vs. a different treat each time).
- Habit design is a "super power" that can be used for good or to exploit; access + data + speed make everything more addictive, so builders need ethics and consumers need awareness.

## Specific tactics (concrete, actionable; include numbers, prices, timings, exact UI choices)
- Trigger: start with external triggers (email, link, app icon) and build association with an internal trigger ("I'm bored" -> Facebook; "what's going on in the world?" -> Twitter).
- Action: use the two levers of motivation and ability (Fogg); make the intended action as easy as possible (one click on an intriguing photo takes Barbra to Pinterest).
- Variable reward: mix relevant and irrelevant, beautiful and plain items so the brain keeps hunting (Pinterest feed: 45 minutes of scrolling); rewards of tribe/hunt/self.
- Investment: after the reward, ask for time, data, effort, social capital or money that improves the service next time (collect, follow, pin, re-pin); investments make the trigger more engaging, the action easier, the reward more exciting.
- Diagnostic questions (from the 2015 version): what internal trigger, what external trigger, what simplest behavior, is the reward fulfilling yet leaves wanting more, what investment loads the next trigger.

## Contradictions or caveats (where this disagrees with common advice or other videos)
- Eyal insists variable reward is essential, whereas Albert Cheng's Chess.com example shows a predictable positive reward (encouragement after losses) also lifting retention; Tim Gabe's videos treat the same mechanics as neutral UX laws.
- Eyal's ethics framing ("if it can't be used for evil, it's not a super power") sits between pure growth content and critics who call the Hook model addictive by design.
