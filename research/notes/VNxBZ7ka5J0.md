## Core claims (3-7 bullets, what the video argues)
- Cohort retention is "the best quantitative way to determine if you've made something people want", i.e. the metric for product-market fit.
- Build the metric in three steps: define cohorts (by first-use week/month, optionally by country, acquisition source, device), define the "active" action (not just opening the app), pick a time granularity matched to natural usage frequency.
- The shape of the curve matters more than its height: a curve that flattens means users found durable value and users accumulate; a curve that keeps declining means a leaky bucket and likely no PMF.
- Common pitfalls inflate retention: too-wide time windows, too-shallow actions (app opens, notification taps), counting paying-but-inactive subscribers, and trusting analytics tools blindly.
- Levers to flatten the curve: improve the product (latency, workflow), acquire the right users, segment cohorts to find what works, fix onboarding/activation, and build network effects.
- Talk to churned users as well as retained ones; qualitative feedback tells you what to change.

## Specific tactics (concrete, actionable; include numbers, prices, timings, exact UI choices)
- Choose granularity by use case: a travel app like Airbnb should measure quarterly or semi-annually, a daily-use product weekly; mismatched windows produce false positives.
- Count each user once per cohort period regardless of visits; build a "triangle chart" (rows = signup month, columns = months since signup), normalize to percentages, and read both rows (cohort quality over time) and columns (whether newer cohorts beat older ones).
- Plot cohort curves as lines and look for the flattening; don't judge on a single data point or one week's number.
- Define "active" by picturing a customer getting real value and picking the action that proves it (e.g. completing the core task, not merely opening the app).
- If you charge subscriptions, track payment AND active usage separately (Netflix subscribers who never watch).
- Build the first cohort curves yourself from logs or a spreadsheet before relying on an analytics tool, and verify tool numbers against your own.
- Refresh the cohort graphs weekly or bi-weekly to catch declines early.
- If curves don't flatten, segment by country, customer size, channel etc. to find cohorts that do retain, then acquire more of those users.
- Learn users' pre-existing workflow so onboarding/activation meets them where they are.
- Aim for "smiling" cohort curves that rise over time; visualize with a "layer cake" chart of active users stacked by original cohort in absolute time.

## Named examples (apps/companies cited and what they did)
- Bump (Lieb's startup): measured weekly cohorts, then widened the window and got misleadingly positive numbers; a VC meeting exposed his misunderstanding of cohort retention.
- Google Photos: early cohorts dropped sharply but stabilized at 20-40% retention; that plateau gave the team confidence and the product grew over four years. A marketing push at younger demographics retained poorly because it mismatched the core use case.
- Google+: notification taps created fake engagement without real activity.
- Netflix: subscribers who pay but don't watch illustrate why revenue alone is a bad activity signal.
- Airbnb: example of low-frequency usage requiring long measurement periods.

## Contradictions or caveats (where this disagrees with common advice or other videos)
- Argues against DAU/MAU-style vanity engagement and against "opens" as activity, which many growth dashboards default to.
- Says a lower-but-flat curve (Product B) can beat a higher-but-declining curve (Product A), contradicting the instinct to optimize early retention numbers.
- Recommends spreadsheets over analytics tools at first, contrary to the usual "instrument everything with a tool" advice.
