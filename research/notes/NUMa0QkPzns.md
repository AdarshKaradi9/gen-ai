# It's not you. Phones are designed to be addicting.
- URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NUMa0QkPzns | Channel: Vox | Views: 3177224 | Length: 349s
- Sources used: https://rosetta.to/u/vox/it-s-not-you-phones-are-designed-to-be-addicting (full caption transcript); https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20180227-smartphones-design-elements/ (article recap of the video); https://marcus-miller-wadg.squarespace.com/blog/2018/2/26/its-not-you-phones-are-designed-to-be-addicting (Vox's own blurb reposted)
- Confidence: high

## Core claims (3-7 bullets, what the video argues)
- Phones are intentionally engineered to keep you engaged because tech companies profit from attention, not from your wellbeing ("it's not designed to help us, it's just designed to keep us hooked").
- Three design elements do most of the work: unpredictable (variable-reward) notifications, color, and infinite scroll / autoplay with no stopping cues.
- Tristan Harris (ex-Google design ethicist, Time Well Spent) offers three counter-measures: turn off non-human notifications, greyscale the screen, restrict the home screen to in-the-moment tools.
- Apps simulate social interaction (e.g., Facebook "a friend is interested in an event near you") to pull you back; this is "puppet master" design.
- People rely on external visual cues, not internal satisfaction, to stop consuming; removing endpoints (infinite scroll, autoplay) removes the stop signal.

## Specific tactics (concrete, actionable; include numbers, prices, timings, exact UI choices)
- Variable rewards: a notification grab-bag that is sometimes good, sometimes bad is what makes checking addictive; "if it wasn't random... you would not get addicted."
- Pull-to-refresh is a deliberate slot-machine lever: apps could auto-update but the pull gives an "addicting illusion of control".
- Bundled notifications at set times reduce stress (research cited; Gigazine recap specifies subjects receiving notifications 3 times a day showed lower stress).
- Color: human eyes gravitate to warm colors, especially bright red in eye-tracking; that is why icons got "brighter, bolder and warmer" and why notification badges are red. Fix: enable greyscale color filter in accessibility settings.
- Home screen: keep only tools you use to live in the moment (Lyft, Maps, Calendar); push anything with infinite scroll off it.
- Infinite scroll and autoplay remove the built-in endpoint that pagination provides.

## Named examples (apps/companies cited and what they did)
- Facebook: push notifications about friends' event interest to leverage desire for social connection.
- BlackBerry (2003): push email introduced so you'd check the phone LESS; now notifications come from every app.
- Slot machines: make more money in the US than baseball, movies and theme parks combined; addict people 3-4x faster than other gambling.
- Instagram/Airbnb-style icon redesigns toward bolder, warmer colors (mentioned in recaps).
- Time Well Spent (Harris's nonprofit, later Center for Humane Technology).

## Numbers & evidence (any stats, revenue, conversion rates, retention figures; mark self-reported vs third-party)
- 2.5 billion smartphone users (video, third-party).
- Slot machines: 3-4x faster to addict than other gambling; revenue greater than baseball + movies + theme parks combined (video).
- 2005 self-refilling soup bowl study: people ate 73% more and felt no more satisfied (Wansink study cited by video).
- "Most of us drastically underestimate how often we check our phones."

## Contradictions or caveats (where this disagrees with common advice or other videos)
- The video frames the exact same mechanics (variable reward, red badges, pull-to-refresh, infinite feed) that growth videos in this corpus recommend as "hooks"; it's the ethical mirror image of Hooked-style advice.
- Notification advice is nuanced: notifications from real humans are fine; the problem is app-generated ones.
- Harris acknowledges the deeper question ("what is genuinely worth your attention on an interrupted basis") has no settled answer.
