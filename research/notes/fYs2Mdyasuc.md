## Core claims (3-7 bullets, what the video argues)
- The video is a narrated tour of Jon Yablonski's Laws of UX site: 19 named principles, each with its origin and how to apply it to interface design, in ~30 seconds each.
- Most interface debates (button size, menu depth, how much to show) were settled by decades-old psychology research; designers should apply evidence, not taste.
- Perception (Gestalt), decision load (Hick, Miller), memory (serial position, Zeigarnik, Von Restorff) and motor/response mechanics (Fitts, Doherty) together explain why "intuitive" interfaces feel intuitive.
- Simplicity principles (Prägnanz, Occam, Tesler) say complexity can be moved to the system but not eliminated, so defaults and progressive disclosure matter.

## Specific tactics (concrete, actionable; include numbers, prices, timings, exact UI choices)
The 19 laws in the video's order (timestamps from the description), with the definition from lawsofux.com and the application notes from the written guides:
1. 00:23 Aesthetic-Usability Effect: users perceive attractive design as more usable (Kurosu & Kashimura 1995, 26 ATM layouts). Polish the first impression; looks never excuse broken function.
2. 00:50 Doherty Threshold: keep system response under 400ms (Doherty & Thadani, IBM 1982); use loading indicators/optimistic UI when you cannot.
3. 01:25 Fitts's Law: time to hit a target depends on distance and size; make primary buttons large and near the current focus, within thumb reach on mobile.
4. 02:02 Hick's Law: decision time grows with number/complexity of options (Hick 1952, Hyman 1953); break tasks into steps, trim menus, hide advanced options.
5. 02:39 Jakob's Law: users spend most time on other sites and expect yours to work the same; innovate on top of conventions (cart icons, hamburger menus, swipe gestures).
6. 03:07 Law of Common Region: elements inside a shared boundary are grouped; cards, panels and boxes.
7. 03:40 Law of Prägnanz: ambiguous images are read in the simplest form; icons must read at a glance.
8. 04:21 Law of Proximity: nearby objects are grouped; keep labels next to inputs, separate sections with whitespace.
9. 04:38 Law of Similarity: elements sharing color/shape/size are read as related; style all primary buttons the same.
10. 05:00 Law of Uniform Connectedness: visually connected elements (lines, containers) are perceived as more related.
11. 05:20 Miller's Law: working memory holds 7 +/- 2 items; chunk content (phone numbers 020 1234 5678).
12. 05:45 Occam's Razor: among equally good solutions choose the one with fewest assumptions; remove complexity that obscures core value.
13. 06:04 Pareto Principle: 80% of effects from 20% of causes; concentrate design effort on the vital fifth of functionality.
14. 06:38 Parkinson's Law: tasks inflate to fill available time; constrain timeframes and flows.
15. 07:02 Postel's Law: be liberal in what you accept, conservative in what you send; accept phone numbers with or without spaces, forgive formatting.
16. 07:29 Serial Position Effect: first and last items are remembered best; put critical nav items first and the desired action last.
17. 08:04 Tesler's Law (conservation of complexity): some complexity cannot be removed, only moved; absorb it with smart defaults, progressive disclosure, automation.
18. 08:42 Von Restorff Effect (isolation effect): the one thing that differs is remembered; use contrast for the primary CTA, and spend emphasis sparingly.
19. 09:13 Zeigarnik Effect: incomplete tasks are remembered better; progress bars, partially completed profiles and completion percentages pull users to finish onboarding.
- Not in the 19 but on the source site: Peak-End Rule, Goal-Gradient Effect, Flow, Cognitive Load, Choice Overload, Selective Attention, Working Memory, Mental Model, Paradox of the Active User.

## Contradictions or caveats (where this disagrees with common advice or other videos)
- Aesthetic-Usability Effect cuts against "function over looks" advice such as Kole Jain's video: attractive design is perceived as more usable even when it is not, which is both a tool and a trap (it masks usability problems in testing).
- Jakob's Law argues against novel layouts; Tesler's Law warns that "simplify" often just pushes complexity onto the user.
- Zeigarnik/Goal-Gradient are the psychological basis for streaks and progress bars discussed in the Duolingo episode; the video presents them neutrally without the "don't cheapen it" caveats.
- The "19 principles" count is specific to the site at the time of recording; later versions list more, and Miller's Law is often misapplied as a hard design limit.
