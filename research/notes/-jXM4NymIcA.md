## Core claims (3-7 bullets, what the video argues)
- Companies' economic value is increasingly "a function of the strength of the habits they create"; amassing users is not enough.
- The Hook Model: Trigger -> Action -> Variable Reward -> Investment. Cycling through hooks moves users from external triggers (emails, icons, notifications) to internal triggers (emotions, routines) so they self-cue without marketing.
- A habit is "an impulse to act on a behavior with little or no conscious thought" (Medium recap snippet). "It's the product that captures the monopoly of the mind, the product that we turn to with little or no conscious thought out of habit" that wins, not the best technology (Modern CTO).
- "The ads don't bring people in; the experiences themselves create habits" (Medium recap snippet). Habit-forming products manufacture desire via product experience rather than advertising.
- Behaviour requires motivation, ability and a trigger at the same moment (B = MAT, Fogg's model, referenced in the recap snippet).
- Habits are a "super power" that can be used for good or to exploit; designers and consumers both need to understand the mechanics.

## Specific tactics (concrete, actionable; include numbers, prices, timings, exact UI choices)
- Trigger: start with external triggers (email, link, app icon, push) and pair them with a specific internal trigger - an emotion or routine ("I'm bored" -> Facebook; "what's going on in the world?" -> Twitter). The goal is first-to-mind association.
- Action: make the intended behaviour as easy as possible while boosting motivation (usability design); the simplest action done in anticipation of a reward (click a photo, scroll, search).
- Variable reward: predictable feedback loops do not create desire ("the fridge light"); variability "multiplies the effect" and creates a hunting state. Mix relevant and irrelevant, beautiful and plain items (Pinterest feed example: Barbra spends 45 minutes scrolling). Classic examples: slot machines, lotteries.
- Investment: after the reward, ask the user for a bit of work - time, data, effort, social capital or money - that improves the product for the next cycle (invite friends, state preferences, build virtual assets, learn features; Pinterest follow/pin/re-pin). Investment loads the next trigger and makes the next action easier and the reward better.
- Diagnostic from Modern CTO: "Research is mesearch" - study the products that hook you; map your own product's four steps and look for the weakest.
- Startup Grind description: the talk also covers "traction and DIS-traction", internal vs external triggers and how to "hack back" devices - the user-side flip of the same model.

## Contradictions or caveats (where this disagrees with common advice or other videos)
- Eyal frames habit design as morally neutral and demands it be used only for products that improve lives - a tension with growth-at-all-cost framings.
- Ad-driven acquisition (AppsFlyer-style paid UA) is explicitly downgraded: "the ads don't bring people in"; habits form from the experience.
