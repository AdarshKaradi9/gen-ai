## Core claims (3-7 bullets, what the video argues)
- Onboarding is "a first impression, but it's also so much more than that. It can make or break how successful users are with your product, not just in their first visit, but in every visit after."
- The "golden rule of user onboarding": if you ask the user to do something, don't just tell them why, show them how it improves their experience.
- Asks without context (email, location, data access, notifications) cause hesitation or rejection: "Why do you need my location? ... what do I as the user get in return?"
- Speed is not the goal; confidence in the outcome is. A fast flow that produces generic results scores badly in usability testing.
- Test onboarding with usability studies that measure user confidence, not just completion rate or time-to-complete.

## Specific tactics (concrete, actionable; include numbers, prices, timings, exact UI choices)
- Pair every ask with a user-facing benefit line. Notion's examples: "Notion is 50% faster in the desktop app" next to the download prompt; "Sync your calendar to seamlessly organize your projects and schedule" next to the calendar-sync prompt.
- Structure: a step-by-step personalization flow with clear copy; first couple of steps ask what the user is interested in and suggest ways to use the product, then the "do something" steps (download app, sync calendar) come after.
- Defer account creation until the moment of value. In the Triply redesign the email signup was removed from step 1 and reappears only when the user tries to save an activity to their agenda, with the copy "Save the activities you love and keep your perfect trip plan all in one place."
- Add personalization questions that visibly feed the output: "Where are you going?" -> "How long are you staying?" (with an explanation that length of stay is used to recommend fitting activities) -> "Who are you traveling with?" (solo / partner / friends / family, with explanation) -> more detailed interest tags.
- Testing protocol: run a Lyssna usability study where participants walk the onboarding and then answer a confidence question about the recommendations; run the identical study on the redesign and compare.

## Named examples (apps/companies cited and what they did)
- Notion: post-signup personalization questions, then benefit-framed prompts to download the desktop app and sync calendar.
- Duolingo: asks for email only after the first lesson, explaining it saves progress "so you don't lose your streak."
- Yelp: asks for location, explaining it shows nearby places and events you'll care about.
- Depop: asks to enable notifications, explaining it's the fastest way to know when a price drops on a liked item.
- Triply (a hypothetical travel app for New Orleans trip planning): original flow = signup first -> destination -> a few interests -> recommendations; redesigned as described above.

## Numbers & evidence (any stats, revenue, conversion rates, retention figures; mark self-reported vs third-party)
- "Notion is 50% faster in the desktop app" (Notion's own in-product claim, quoted by the presenter).
- Usability study results are described qualitatively only: original Triply flow = "most users weren't very confident" in recommendations; redesign = "the results flipped. Users felt way more confident." No percentages given (Lyssna's own study, self-reported).

## Contradictions or caveats (where this disagrees with common advice or other videos)
- Pushes back on "shorter onboarding is always better": the redesign ADDS questions (length of stay, travel companions, more interest tags) and improves perceived quality.
- Argues against measuring onboarding by completion rate or time-to-complete alone; recommends measuring confidence/"set up for success."
- The product is Lyssna's own tool, so the testing recommendation doubles as a product pitch.

## Memorable framings (quotable one-liners or named frameworks, verbatim if available)
