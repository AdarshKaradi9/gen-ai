# Value Props: Create a Product People Will Actually Buy

## Core claims (3-7 bullets, what the video argues)
- The #1 reason startups fail is that they are not solving a valuable enough problem; ideas are meaningless unless they solve a real, painful problem. "Fall in love with the problem, not your solution."
- A value proposition must be defined for a specific "minimum viable segment" (MVS), never "everyone"; if your user and your buyer are different people you must satisfy both.
- Qualify the problem with the 4U test (Unworkable, Unavoidable, Urgent, Underserved); if it does not hit most of the 4Us you are building a vitamin, not a painkiller.
- Qualify the solution with the 3D test (Disruptive, Discontinuous, Defensible). Do not compete on "better, faster, cheaper".
- People only switch if the Gain/Pain ratio is overwhelming: the gain must be ~10x the pain of adopting (learning curve, switching risk, integration, inertia, cost).
- Write the value prop in a fixed template and evaluate it with a before/after picture of the customer's life.

## Specific tactics (concrete, actionable; include numbers, prices, timings, exact UI choices)
- Value-prop template (from the lecture): "For (target customer) who (is dissatisfied with current alternative / has unmet need), our (product) is a (category) that (provides key benefit). Unlike (competitor / status quo), we (unique 3D differentiation)."
- Minimum Viable Segment: pick a segment small enough that all members share the same need and use the product the same way; resist stretching one product across many user needs.
- 4U checklist questions: Unworkable = does the current situation cause severe consequences / job loss / major inefficiency (example in talk: iPhone activation failures)? Unavoidable = regulatory/compliance/forced (taxes, aging, education, HIPAA)? Urgent = is this a top-3 priority right now; "let's talk in six months" means not urgent? Underserved = high demand, no viable solution (example: affordable robotics toys in Africa)?
- 3D checklist: Disruptive (changes the industry's business model, e.g. Airbnb shared housing, SaaS pricing vs perpetual licenses); Discontinuous (enables something previously impossible, 10x not 10%, e.g. AWS making cloud viable); Defensible (patents, proprietary data moat, network effects, unique algorithms).
- Gain/Pain: enumerate pains beyond price (learning curve, disruption risk, failure risk, inertia, integration, training). Target ratio: ~10:1 for B2B; for consumers Skok cites a 30-90% cost saving as the kind of gain that moves people. "Likelihood of purchase = Gain / Pain."
- Before/After framework (at ~1:09 in video): write the "before" state (pain points, lost productivity) and the "after" state (gains) explicitly; example used is digital menopause solutions (before: discomfort and lost productivity; after: regained health and efficiency).
- ICP questions: Who feels the 4U pain most acutely? Who is the economic buyer (often not the user)? Who are the early adopters desperate enough to tolerate an unpolished product?
- Spend "twice as long understanding the problem as designing the solution" (write-up's paraphrase of the lecture's advice).

## Named examples (apps/companies cited and what they did)
- Rent the Runway: solves an underserved fashion need with urgent demand (4U example).
- Venmo: early pains were security and small network, but convenience won the gain/pain trade.
- Airbnb (disruptive business model), AWS (discontinuous), Apple App Store/iPad (third-party apps turned it into a must-have), Uber (10x reimagining of rides, in write-up).
- Nonprofit providing digital literacy to marginalized children in Kazakhstan (audience example used for "for who"); Black student graduation rates; space health for astronauts (urgent for travelers, not the general public).
- Netflix recommendation data moat, Adobe Creative Cloud subscription shift, Slack and Stripe value-prop statements (illustrations in the calmops write-up, not necessarily verbatim from the lecture).

## Contradictions or caveats (where this disagrees with common advice or other videos)
- Skok's bar is explicitly anti-"better, faster, cheaper"; this contrasts with marketing videos that treat incremental differentiation as enough.
- Skok's 10x gain requirement is stricter than "launch something bad quickly" (Seibel) - the two agree on starting narrow, but Skok front-loads problem validation before building.
- The calmops write-up adds examples (Slack, Stripe, Netflix, Adobe) that may be the author's illustrations rather than Skok's; treat them as secondary.
