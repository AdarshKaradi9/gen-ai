## Core claims (3-7 bullets, what the video argues)
- A montage of S23-batch founders answering "how did you get your first customer?" at the start of the batch. The honest spread: cold outreach, door-to-door, personal networks, friends and family, waitlists on Reddit, viral Twitter/GitHub launches, advisors' intros, and several who "don't have them yet."
- Most first customers come from people the founders already know or can reach directly; "as with a lot of founders, your first few customers come from your network."
- Selling before building works: one team sold to one company for $250/month with no code and shipped a rough version in seven days.
- Virality is rare and lucky ("lightning in a bottle"); the median story is unglamorous hustle.

## Specific tactics (concrete, actionable; include numbers, prices, timings, exact UI choices)
- Cold email ("surprisingly a cold email"); "a lot of calls and emailing on Reddit"; "very crafted cold outreach."
- Door-to-door: waited in the sun for a food-truck owner to take a break, then pitched and closed on the spot.
- Voicemail to a physical therapist; she called back; the founders explained their personal reason for building it and she bought because "we were coming from a good place."
- Customer discovery calls that turned into a sale: sold to one company for $250 for a month before any code existed, built a rough version in 7 days and deployed it.
- LinkedIn: found the first customer on LinkedIn, then used conversations to understand needs.
- Reddit waitlist: made a mock-up of a "dream personal finance product," a website with a waitlist, posted to their own Reddit group; ~1,000 signups within 24 hours; then launched a closed beta and onboarded those users.
- Twitter + GitHub launch: in a few days went from 50 to ~10,000 followers, 20,000 GitHub stars, 150,000 unique visitors a day; customers came inbound.
- Shared what they were building on their own LinkedIns ~2.5 weeks earlier; inbound waitlist; first paying customer "this morning" from that waitlist.
- Advisor introductions into big ag-tech companies; flying to LA to "make the rounds of the production companies" (data for film/TV).
- Repurposing inbound recruiter messages: companies asking to hire him as a biotech software engineer get the reply "No, but how would you like to buy software that does the same thing."
- Launch with friends and family to test processes and pricing, then let referrals evolve from there; one founder's in-laws' holiday home was the first install.
- Convert existing free users into paid.

## Named examples (apps/companies cited and what they did)
- Founders are unnamed in the montage. Platforms named: Reddit, Twitter, GitHub, LinkedIn. Sectors mentioned: physical therapy, food trucks, personal finance, ag tech, film/TV production data, biotech software, home systems.

## Numbers & evidence (any stats, revenue, conversion rates, retention figures; mark self-reported vs third-party)
- $250/month first contract with no code, built in 7 days (self-reported).
- ~1,000 waitlist signups in 24 hours from a Reddit post (self-reported).
- 50 -> ~10,000 Twitter followers, 20,000 GitHub stars, 150,000 unique visitors/day within days of launch (self-reported).
- Several founders had zero customers at the time of filming (start of S23 batch).

## Contradictions or caveats (where this disagrees with common advice or other videos)
- Several founders openly have no customers yet, which tempers the "everyone has traction" narrative around YC.
- Friends-and-family and network sales are presented as legitimate first customers; some advice warns these do not validate demand.
- The viral GitHub/Twitter story is presented as luck, not a repeatable tactic.
- No single playbook: it contradicts any video claiming one channel (ASO, cold email, build in public) is the way.
