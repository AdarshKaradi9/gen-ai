## Core claims (3-7 bullets, what the video argues)
- Fast-growing bootstrapped SaaS products are not just better products; they "figured out how to blow up their user base and how to keep up momentum" — "this is not just luck, this is science" and can be reproduced.
- Every user acquisition has three elements: a method, a channel, and a cost. All online marketing falls into four methods: incoming (inbound), outgoing (outbound), referrals, and advertising (paid inbound).
- A three-stage roadmap: Stage 1 first 5 then 100 users (outbound then referral), Stage 2 500-1,000 users (inbound content), Stage 3 1,000+ (ads plus affiliate program), with the early stages' models still running.
- The first five users are the most important: serve them so well they become advocates.
- Stage 2 "is long and hard"; results compound slowly; let revenue from existing users pay for acquiring new ones.
- Before any of this you need a "minimum lovable product," not just an MVP (podcast source).

## Specific tactics (concrete, actionable; include numbers, prices, timings, exact UI choices)
- Model 1 (first 5 users): method outgoing; channel email/DMs; cost $0. Reach your close network (family, friends, colleagues, partners); say you have an MVP and want first users; offer a free account for life in exchange only for feedback. Put all five in a small community (Discord, Facebook group, WhatsApp). Spend time fixing bugs and building their requested features so they become advocates. Podcast adds: for high-friction products, even consider paying your first users; FeedHive's early community was a 20-person Twitter chat group.
- Model 2 (5 -> 100 users): method referrals; channel referral links held by early users; cost $0 (he argues a revenue share is usually unnecessary if the first five feel like part of the project). Instead give new users a big early-joiner discount so advocates have "a really good offer on their hand" (podcast: a voucher or half-off code and the framing "I'm a partner in this project").
- Model 3 (Stage 2, 500-1,000 users): method incoming; channels social media and search. Long-form video for YouTube search, blog posts for Google, TikToks/Reels/Shorts for attention, LinkedIn updates on highs and lows and milestones, lessons on Hacker News and Indie Hackers; keep going through long stretches of no engagement; budget content spend against ARPU. Podcast: allocate one to two years for this stage to reach roughly 200-1,000 paying users.
- Model 4 (Stage 3, ads): turn the best-performing Stage 2 content into paid ads; channels Google Ads (search + YouTube video), Meta (Facebook/Instagram image + video), TikTok video. Growth mode: ignore return and max revenue into acquisition; profit mode: aim for 3:1 ROAS ($1 spent -> $3 earned -> $1 reinvested, $1 profit). Keep CAC below average LTV computed from churn; in growth mode you can spend nearly the full LTV per acquisition.
- Model 5 (Stage 3, affiliates): a public affiliate program with self-serve referral links; compensation seen in the wild at 10%, 20%, even 50% of recurring revenue; in growth mode share a big chunk, in automation mode go as low as gift cards, feature unlocks, or higher usage limits.
- Roadmap can be reordered (ads early, outbound late) but this is the sequence he used for three products.

## Numbers & evidence (any stats, revenue, conversion rates, retention figures; mark self-reported vs third-party)
- Stage targets: 5 -> 100 -> 500-1,000 -> 1,000+ users (self-defined thresholds).
- 3:1 ROAS target in profit mode (rule of thumb).
- Affiliate payout ranges observed: 10-50% of recurring revenue (third-party observation).
- Self-reported (podcast): three SaaS products built with this system, combined 1M+ ARR; 100K YouTube subscribers; one to two years for Stage 2; 200-1,000 paying users from organic content.
- No conversion or retention percentages are given; cost decisions are tied to ARPU and LTV rather than stated numbers.

## Contradictions or caveats (where this disagrees with common advice or other videos)
- Argues against paying early referrers or revenue-sharing at the 5->100 stage, whereas many referral playbooks start with incentives; he moves incentives to the new user instead.
- Deliberately delays paid ads until after ~1,000 users and known LTV, unlike creators who run ads from day one; also says outbound sales at scale is "not really a self-serve SaaS anymore" and suits VC-backed teams.
- Giving lifetime-free accounts (or even paying users) to the first cohort contradicts the "never devalue your product" school; he says it "accurately values" how important those users are.
- Stage 2 is admitted to be slow (years), which contrasts with the viral-in-weeks app stories elsewhere in this batch.
