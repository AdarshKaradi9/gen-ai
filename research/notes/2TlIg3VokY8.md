# The UX Psychology Behind Apps People Can't Stop Using

- Sources used: https://youtubesummary.com/summary/2TlIg3VokY8 (detailed AI summary with the six principles and before/after examples); https://videohighlight.com/v/2TlIg3VokY8 (chaptered AI summary of the same video)
- Confidence: medium (two detailed summaries agree on all six principles and the numbers; no verbatim transcript read)

## Core claims (3-7 bullets, what the video argues)
- Most app failures come from ignoring user psychology, not from bad aesthetics; great designers understand how users think.
- Six psychological principles, each shown as a before/after screen redesign: (1) decision fatigue / smart defaults, (2) goal-gradient effect, (3) reciprocity, (4) IKEA/endowment effect, (5) loss framing over gain framing, (6) contrast/anchoring in pricing.
- Closing synthesis: "defaults feel like recommendations, initial numbers set anchors, gifts create debts, building fosters ownership, and visible progress generates momentum."

## Specific tactics (concrete, actionable; include numbers, prices, timings, exact UI choices)
- Kill blank forms: every empty field is a decision. Pre-fill with the most common answers so the user "scans and adjusts" instead of filling from scratch; set expectations in the button copy (e.g. button text saying results are already available). Users read defaults as recommendations.
- Never start progress at 0%: show the first onboarding step already checked (e.g. "20% complete") so the user feels underway. LinkedIn's profile-strength meter never starts at zero.
- Give before you ask: instead of "Create account / enter email to unlock results", show a usable partial report (what is wrong, top issues) immediately, then ask to save/upgrade. Analogues cited: Costco samples, Spotify Premium trial, Notion full trial.
- Make signup feel like continuing: let users build something first (choose name, title, colours, styles), then replace the "Sign up" button with "Continue". Duolingo asks for language, goals and runs the first lesson before the account screen.
- Flip upgrade prompts to loss framing: instead of "Upgrade now / Maybe later", show what they will lose (their files, a countdown) if they do nothing; make dismissing feel like accepting the downside.
- Never show a price in isolation: a $50/month protection plan alone becomes "$600/year" in the user's head; show it right after a $1,900 laptop and label it as a small relative figure (e.g. "2.6%"). The first number seen becomes the ruler.

## Numbers & evidence (any stats, revenue, conversion rates, retention figures; mark self-reported vs third-party)
- Jam study (attributed in video to Columbia): 24 options → 3% bought; 6 options → 30% bought (third-party academic study, Iyengar & Lepper, cited secondhand).
- Car-wash loyalty card (attributed to Columbia): cards with two stamps pre-filled were completed at nearly double the rate (goal-gradient study, cited secondhand).
- Free samples "up to 2,000% purchase lift" (video's claim, source not given).
- Kahneman: losses feel roughly twice as powerful as gains (cited).
- Cialdini cited for reciprocity as one of the most powerful persuasion levers.
- No first-party conversion data from uxpeak itself.

## Contradictions or caveats (where this disagrees with common advice or other videos)
- Several of these are the mechanics other videos in the corpus flag as dark patterns (loss framing, anchoring); the video presents them neutrally as "psychology", not ethics.
- The reciprocity principle (show value before the account wall) is in tension with the Cravotta/Cal AI pattern of long questionnaires and hard paywalls before any product use; Mobbin's study (Qsq-Sj_rojU) sides with uxpeak here (Alma lets you try the core experience before signup).
- Promotional: roughly a third of the summary is Mobbin and uxpeak+ course pitches.
